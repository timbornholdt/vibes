# Name five resident and five migratory birds in your area.

## Short answer
**Residents (here all year):** Black-capped Chickadee, Northern Cardinal, Blue Jay, Downy Woodpecker, White-breasted Nuthatch.
**Migratory:** Bobolink (summer breeder), Baltimore Oriole (summer breeder), Ruby-throated Hummingbird (summer breeder), Sandhill Crane (spring/fall passage and local breeder), Dark-eyed Junco (winter visitor from the north), Snow Bunting (winter visitor of open fields).
More are in the tables below. Honest note: the species lists come from well-established regional bird knowledge and the sources listed; I could not load eBird's bar charts in this session, so the exact weeks are approximate until you check them (see verification steps).

## The details

### What "resident" and "migratory" mean here
- **Resident:** a species that you can find in the Rosemount area in every month, essentially the same individuals or population. Some "residents" shift a little in winter (for example, some cardinals or Canada Geese move short distances), but the species never leaves.
- **Summer breeder / long-distance migrant:** arrives in spring, nests, leaves by fall (to the tropics or the southern U.S.).
- **Winter visitor:** breeds in Canada or northern Minnesota, arrives October-November, leaves March-April.
- **Pass-through migrant:** present in Rosemount only for days or weeks in spring and fall while travelling to or from breeding grounds farther north.
- Rosemount sits on the **Mississippi Flyway**, and the Mississippi/Vermillion/St. Croix river valleys concentrate migrants.

### What the sources confirm
- The **Trumpeter Swan** account in the Minnesota Breeding Bird Atlas calls it a widespread year-round resident, with most birds staying through winter where open water remains. It disappeared from Minnesota in the late 1800s and was reintroduced starting in 1966; Minnesota's population was about 17,000 in 2015.
- The **Bobolink** account says it is a regular breeding species and long-distance migrant that winters in South America, favours grasslands, hayfields and CRP fields, is a DNR Species in Greatest Conservation Need, and has declined about 1.9% a year in Minnesota since 1967.
- The Hastings bird brochure lists, for sites around Dakota County's east side, migrating warblers and songbirds at Spring Lake Park Reserve, migrating waterfowl, gulls and shorebirds at Lake Byllesby, and wintering Trumpeter Swans, ducks and gulls at Point Douglas County Park. It also names breeders such as Baltimore Oriole, Eastern Bluebird, Dickcissel, Sandhill Crane, Bald Eagle, Pileated Woodpecker and others.
- The **Audubon summary of the Minnesota Christmas Bird Count** includes Lapland Longspurs, Snow Buntings and Horned Larks among the winter field birds statewide, and notes five Eurasian Tree Sparrows at the Hastings count (a rarity worth knowing about locally).
- The **Vermillion Highlands** page describes a prairie seeded from 2015 intended to attract pollinators and "prairie bird species," so it is a natural spot for Bobolinks, meadowlarks and sparrows in summer and Rough-legged Hawks or Snow Buntings nearby in winter.

### The "pass-through" gap
Many people think a bird is either here or not. At Rosemount, a third group matters: **neotropical warblers, thrushes and sparrows** that appear in a few days of mid-May and again late August to mid-October on their way between the tropics and Canadian forests. Those are the birds you notice are suddenly "everywhere" after a south wind.

## How to find and verify this yourself
1. **eBird bar charts (best tool).** Go to ebird.org > Explore > Explore Regions > type "Dakota County, Minnesota" > **Bar Charts**. Each row is a species; the thickness of the bar in each of 48 weeks (4 per month) is the frequency with which the species appears on checklists. A bar present in all 48 weeks = year-round resident. A bar that is missing in winter = summer breeder. A bar missing in summer = winter visitor. Short bars in two spots (spring and fall) = pass-through migrant. Click a species name to see a map, and click the bar to see records.
2. **Hotspot-level view.** On the same site go to Explore > Hotspots and search for hotspots around Rosemount. The eBird-based Dakota County hotspot list I found names Spring Lake Park Reserve (Schaar's Bluff and Camp Spring Lake), Schwarz Pond Park, Lebanon Hills Regional Park, Whitetail Woods Regional Park, and Lake Byllesby Regional Park among the busiest spots. Open a hotspot and choose Bar Charts to get a local pattern.
3. **Check your own backyard.** Use the eBird app (or Merlin Bird ID, a free app from the Cornell Lab) to start a checklist in your yard. Merlin's "Sound ID" is excellent for dawn songs in May.
4. **Christmas Bird Count.** The National Audubon Society runs CBCs each December 14-January 5. Check the Audubon CBC site for the Hastings, St. Paul and Bloomington circles and Minnesota Ornithologists' Union (moumn.org) for circle details to learn which winter birds are routinely recorded.
5. **Minnesota Breeding Bird Atlas (mnbirdatlas.org).** Search any species for a map of blocks where breeding was confirmed (2009-2013). Pick Dakota County blocks to see which of the "summer" birds actually nest near you.
6. **MOU records.** The Minnesota Ornithologists' Union (moumn.org) has the state checklist with status, and its Rare Bird Alert archive reports unusual sightings in Dakota County.
7. **In person, this week (early November).** Look for Sandhill Crane flocks passing overhead (loud rolling calls), Dark-eyed Juncos arriving under feeders, and the first Snow Buntings or Rough-legged Hawks in open farmland. Compare to your first-of-spring checklist.

Caveats: eBird frequencies reflect where people bird, not birds; the Rosemount area is a mix of suburban, farmland and river-valley, so the species list of Dakota County as a whole will include more wetland and bluff birds than a suburban yard.


## Uncertainties and caveats
- I could not load eBird bar charts or species pages in this session, so months in the tables are approximate regional norms, and species statuses such as "Red-bellied Woodpecker resident" or "Sandhill Crane breeds locally" should be confirmed on the bar chart.
- Several "residents" are partial migrants (Blue Jay, Mourning Dove, Canada Goose, American Crow), so there is a legitimate argument about which species count as year-round.
- Bird ranges are shifting with climate; for example Red-bellied Woodpeckers and Northern Cardinals have expanded northward.
- The Eurasian Tree Sparrow rarity at Hastings is from a single CBC summary.
