# Trace the water you drink from precipitation to tap.

## Short answer
Your water is 100% groundwater. It falls as rain or snow, infiltrates through the glacial sediments, leaks down through the bedrock layers above, and reaches the **Jordan Sandstone** aquifer. City wells 400-507 ft deep pump it up; it is chlorinated, fluoridated, treated for iron and manganese with polyphosphate (a sequestering agent, not removal), stored in elevated water towers, and distributed to taps. After you use it, it flows by sewer to the Met Council's **Empire plant** (Empire Township), is treated, and is discharged into the **Mississippi River**.

## The details

### 1. Precipitation and recharge
Rosemount gets roughly 30 inches of precipitation per year (see question 04 for measured numbers). Only a fraction becomes groundwater; most is used by plants or runs off. Rosemount sits on a glacial outwash plain of sand and gravel, which lets water infiltrate more easily than the clay-rich till elsewhere in the metro. Per the city's wellhead protection plan, the shallow glacial/St. Peter aquifer is recharged by infiltrating precipitation (and leakage from lakes and rivers), while deeper bedrock aquifers like the Jordan are recharged mainly by leakage from the units above and beside them. The plan found no direct hydraulic connection between surface water and the city's aquifer. (The city also recommends rain gardens and infiltration practices to support recharge.)

### 2. The rock column beneath you
From the wellhead protection plan (Part II, 2012), approximate layers going down:
- Glacial drift (sand, gravel, clay): about 90-180 ft thick.
- Bedrock below: patches of Platteville Limestone and Glenwood Shale, St. Peter Sandstone (30-40 ft where present), then Prairie du Chien Group dolomite (about 200 ft).
- The Oneota dolomite at the base of the Prairie du Chien acts as a partial barrier (aquitard).
- The top of the **Jordan Sandstone** is more than 320 ft below the surface.
- Groundwater in the area generally flows northeast.
The Dakota County Geologic Atlas, as quoted in the plan, describes the Prairie du Chien-Jordan as confined under most of Rosemount ("leaky confined" for the Jordan).

### 3. Which aquifer
All the city wells listed in the 2012 plan are in the **Jordan Sandstone**; the 2018 Water Supply chapter likewise attributes the east wells (RR #1, #2) to an "East Jordan" source and the seven west wells to a "West Jordan" source. I found no evidence that Rosemount uses Prairie du Chien, Tunnel City (Franconia), or Mt. Simon for city supply. Private wells in the area may tap other units: check the County Well Index.

A Well 17 was planned (site south of Bonaire Path near Akron Avenue); a construction-bid listing shows a "Well 17" project at the conceptual stage as of 2024 with an unspecified location, so I cannot confirm it exists yet. Average daily use in 2018 was about 2.7 MGD; peak day about 6.9 MGD in summer.

### 5. Groundwater age
Tritium (a marker of water that entered the ground after 1953 nuclear testing) was found in city Wells R1, 7, 8, and 9. That means part of the water pumped is less than about 70 years old, so the Jordan here is classified as **vulnerable**: surface activities can reach it within decades. Older components could be far older; I did not find a quantified average age (that would require carbon-14 or modeling in the Geologic Atlas).

### 6. Treatment
Per the 2018 plan, Rosemount had **no central treatment plant**. Each well is chlorinated and fluoridated; polyphosphate is added to keep iron and manganese (aesthetic issues, in Wells 12, 14, 15, 16) from staining. Well 16 was flagged as possibly slightly above the radium standard and blended with Well 14. Future treatment plants were contingency plans. A utility-data aggregator (TapWaterData, secondary source) lists a **gross alpha (radioactivity) health-based violation starting October 2025**, state-archived with no resolution recorded, plus a 2025 sample of 7.2 pCi/L (the limit is 15). I could not verify this on a primary source, so check the current Consumer Confidence Report. The same aggregator reports fluoride 0.83 mg/L and no PFAS above EPA health guidelines.

### 7. Storage and distribution
Four elevated steel tanks hold 3.5 million gallons: Chippendale (1972, 0.5 MG), Connemara (1988, 1.0 MG), Bacardi (2007, 1.5 MG), and East Side (1998, 0.5 MG). There are two pressure zones (West and East). The system has emergency valves to Apple Valley, normally closed. Ask Public Works which zone serves your address.

### 8. After the tap
Rosemount sewage flows through the Rosemount Interceptor to the **Empire Water Resource Recovery Facility**, which serves Apple Valley, Elko New Market, Empire Township, Farmington, Rosemount, and parts of Lakeville, has a 24 MGD capacity, and discharges UV-disinfected treated water to the **Mississippi River**. Stormwater is separate: surface runoff in Rosemount drains toward the Vermillion River (which joins the Mississippi near Hastings) or directly to the Mississippi/Minnesota rivers.

### The whole path in one line
Snow/rain -> sandy outwash soil -> glacial drift -> limestone/dolomite/shale layers -> Jordan Sandstone -> well pump -> chlorine, fluoride, polyphosphate -> water tower -> your tap -> sewer -> Empire plant -> Mississippi River -> Gulf of Mexico.

## How to find and verify this yourself
1. **Consumer Confidence Report (CCR).** Search "Rosemount water quality report" on rosemountmn.gov, or call Public Works at the city (City Hall: 651-322-2003, per a secondary source, verify). Look for the "source of water" section (aquifer name) and the table of detected contaminants (gross alpha/radium, iron, manganese, fluoride, PFAS). CCRs are due by July 1 each year.
2. **MDH Drinking Water Watch.** In the Minnesota Department of Health "Drinking Water Watch" (search for it), enter Rosemount (PWS ID MN1190019) to see wells, sampling results, and violations.
3. **MDH Source Water Protection.** On health.state.mn.us, search "Source Water Protection" and "Rosemount Wellhead Protection Plan"; the city also posts Parts I and II (2010, 2012) in its Document Center, where Table 1 lists each well with its unique number and depth.
4. **County Well Index (CWI).** Use the MN Dept of Health "Minnesota Well Index" viewer: search the unique numbers (e.g., 112212 for Well 7, 457167 for R1, per the plan) to read the driller's log: layers, casing depth, aquifer.
5. **Geologic atlas.** The Dakota County Geologic Atlas (Minnesota Geological Survey, C-06) Part A (geology) and the DNR Part B (hydrogeology) maps show depth to bedrock, aquifer confinement, and sensitivity of the Jordan near your home. DNR "Groundwater Atlas" viewers let you click on a location.
6. **Water Supply Plan.** MN DNR "MPARS" holds Rosemount's Water Supply Plan with demand and well data.
7. **Wastewater.** Met Council's Empire page, and its "Interceptor" map, show the sewer service area. The city's utility staff can confirm which sanitary sewer line your street connects to.
8. **In person.** Look for the water towers (Chippendale, Connemara, Bacardi, East Side) around town. Ask Public Works which pressure zone you are in, and which wells blend to supply your street. Look for well houses (small buildings with fencing) in city parks and utility lots.
9. **Caveats.** City documents date from 2010-2020 and contradict one another on capacity; wells and treatment may have changed since. TapWaterData and similar sites are third-party summaries.


## Uncertainties and caveats
- Well count and treatment status are from 2018-2020 plans; recent changes (Well 17, any new treatment for radium/gross alpha) are unconfirmed.
- The 2025 gross alpha violation comes only from a third-party aggregator.
- Average groundwater age is not published in the sources I could read; only the presence of post-1953 water (tritium).
- Exact pressure zone and well mix at your address are not public.
- I could not read the last ~14% of the two long city documents (they were truncated), so some details (PFAS testing) may appear there.
- Watershed: your neighborhood's stormwater drainage (Vermillion vs Mississippi) was not verified at the parcel scale.
