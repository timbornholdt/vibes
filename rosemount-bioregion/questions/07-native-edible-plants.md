# Name five native edible plants in your region and their season(s) of availability.

## Short answer
Five solid answers for the Rosemount area, in order of the year:

1. **Juneberry / serviceberry** (*Amelanchier*), fruit ripe about late June to July.
2. **Chokecherry** (*Prunus virginiana*), fruit ripe about late July to August (cook it; do not eat it raw).
3. **American hazelnut** (*Corylus americana*), nuts ripe late August to early September.
4. **American plum** (*Prunus americana*), fruit ripe about mid-August to September.
5. **Riverbank wild grape** (*Vitis riparia*), fruit ripe September.

Ready-made alternates: wild strawberry (June), black raspberry (July), elderberry (August), bur oak acorns (Sept to Oct, need leaching), groundnut tubers (fall to early spring, thaw permitting), and sugar maple sap (late Feb to early April).

Today (Nov 6) almost all of these are out of season; the main thing still foragable is groundnut (digging the tubers), and that only if you can positively identify it.

## The details

### Table of candidate plants

"Season" dates are for the Twin Cities / Dakota County area and shift 1 to 2 weeks with the weather year. The sources I could open gave exact ranges only for some plants; the rest are typical ranges from general regional experience and are marked "(typical)". Treat all of them as approximate and check by observing the plants (see below).

Plants I would not use for a quick "Rosemount" answer: ramps (wild leeks) are native but grow in rich woodlands and are slow to recover, so the DNR does not allow harvesting them in state parks.

### Why these seasons
Fruit and nut plants of the prairie-savanna ripen in sequence from June to October, driven by day-length and accumulated heat. A warm spring moves everything earlier by 1 to 2 weeks. The pattern is why Dakota month names refer to ripening fruit: the Dakota name for July is the Red Chokecherry Month and August is the Black Chokecherry Month, as reported by the Devils Lake Journal article cited here.

### Foraging legality and ethics (important)
- **Minnesota state parks and recreation areas:** you may pick edible fruit and mushrooms for personal use, a handful at a time. Digging up or damaging any plant is not allowed, including ramps, ginseng and fiddleheads. Large-scale and commercial harvesting is prohibited.
- **State trails:** edible fruit only.
- **Wildlife Management Areas (for example the Vermillion Highlands WMA):** edible fruits and seeds may be harvested for personal use without a permit; wild ginseng needs a permit; wild rice harvest is open unless posted. Check the posted rules at the site or call the Area Wildlife office.
- **Scientific and Natural Areas:** berry picking is allowed at only eight specific SNAs; the rest are closed to picking.
- **State forests:** small amounts of berries and mushrooms are fine; large amounts need a permit, and tapping maples requires one.
- **County and city parks:** I could not retrieve the text of Dakota County's park ordinance (Ordinance 107) or the Rosemount parks code, so I cannot say what they allow. Many metro park systems ban removing any natural material; Three Rivers Parks, for example, bans collecting plants broadly and says state parks are an exception. Assume it is not allowed unless the park posts otherwise or staff tell you so.
- **Private land and roadsides:** you need permission from the owner, and roadside fruit may have been sprayed with herbicide or sit near road salt and exhaust.
- Take no more than a small share, never uproot a plant, and leave plenty for wildlife. Avoid spreading invasives on boots and tools.

## How to find and verify this yourself
1. **Confirm nativity.** Go to the Minnesota Wildflowers site (minnesotawildflowers.info), search the plant name and check the "Origin" field and the county map. Cross-check with USDA PLANTS (plants.usda.gov): search the scientific name, open the Distribution tab, and look for Dakota County, MN. For a firm county-level record use the Minnesota Biodiversity Atlas (bellmuseum.umn.edu/minnesota-biodiversity-atlas) and search by species and county.
2. **See what is actually growing near you.** In iNaturalist (inaturalist.org), open Explore, type "Rosemount, Minnesota" in the place box (or draw a circle around your neighborhood), type the species name, and use the "Month" histogram to see when it has been observed with fruit. Under "Filters" add the annual-cycle term "Fruiting" to see just the fruit observations. Do this to learn the typical dates for your own area rather than trusting a book.
3. **Confirm the ripening window by watching.** UMN SeasonWatch (seasonwatch.umn.edu) and the USA National Phenology Network's Nature's Notebook let you read other observers' dates, and you can submit your own. Visit a thicket every week from mid-June on and note the dates yourself.
4. **Learn ID from a good book, not an app alone.** Samuel Thayer's *Nature's Garden* and *The Forager's Harvest* are the standard Midwest guides, and the UMN Extension and DNR pages cited below provide more. Photo-ID apps are good for a first guess, not for deciding what is safe to eat.
5. **Know the rules for the exact land.** Check the MN DNR "Harvesting plants" page for state land, and call Dakota County Parks or Rosemount Parks and Recreation for the local rules before picking in any county or city park.
6. **Places to look nearby (verify access and rules first):** Vermillion Highlands WMA / UMore Park area, Lebanon Hills Regional Park, Spring Lake Park Reserve, Whitetail Woods Regional Park, and the Vermillion River corridor. These are suggestions to check, not confirmed foraging spots.

Caveats: apps and websites mislead most when the plant is out of season (no fruit to compare), when there are toxic lookalikes (moonseed and grape; elderberry species), and when planted cultivars are mistaken for wild natives.


## Uncertainties and caveats
- Exact ripening dates vary by year and microsite. Several windows above are typical regional ranges, not figures I could confirm from a Dakota County dataset.
- The juneberry ripening range I could verify came from SeasonWatch photo captions (mid-June green, July 21 ripe in Washington County), not a formal statistic. The SeasonWatch page did not state nativity, though several *Amelanchier* species are native in Minnesota.
- Native language names: only Caŋpa (chokecherry) and Lakota thíŋpsiŋla are sourced. I deliberately left the others blank rather than guess. A Dakota-language dictionary or the Dakota Language Education resources are the right place to fill these.
- Chokecherry safety advice varies; one source says crushing makes the fruit safe and another warns about pits. Use tested recipes and remove pits.
- Prairie turnip and wild rice are native to Minnesota or nearby regions but are weak answers for Rosemount specifically.
- The state of foraging law in Minnesota is in flux (a legislative Sustainable Foraging Task Force has been reviewing the rules). Re-check before you go.
- Wild Ones, a native-plant group, advises against foraging wild plants and prefers buying identified plants for the garden; I include that as a counterweight to the ethic of harvesting.
- County and city park foraging rules were not verified.
