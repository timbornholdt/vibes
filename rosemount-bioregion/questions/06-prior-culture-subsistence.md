# What were the primary subsistence techniques of the culture that lived in your area before you?

## Short answer
Rosemount is in **Mni Sota Makoce**, the Dakota homeland. This stretch of Dakota County was used mainly by the **Mdewakanton (Bdewakantunwan, "Spirit Lake People") Dakota**. Their villages stood along the Mississippi and Minnesota rivers a few miles north of here: Kaposia, Black Dog, and the villages around **Bdote**, the confluence near Mendota and Fort Snelling. They were not nomads and not only farmers. They followed a **seasonal round** that moved people to each food source in its season:

- spring maple sugar camps
- summer village gardens of corn, beans, and squash, plus fishing
- late-summer and fall wild rice and wild plant foods
- fall and winter hunting of deer, plus bison on the prairies to the west and south

They also used **fire** to keep the prairie and oak savanna open. Earlier peoples (Paleoindian, Archaic, Woodland, and Oneota) lived in the same river corridors for more than 10,000 years before that.

The Dakota are a **living people**. Their descendants include the Shakopee Mdewakanton Sioux Community, Prairie Island Indian Community, Lower Sioux Indian Community, and Upper Sioux Community in Minnesota, as well as Dakota nations in the Dakotas, Nebraska, Montana, and Canada. Many of these practices are being carried on or revived today.

## The details

### Who lived here
- **Dakota (Oceti Sakowin, Seven Council Fires)**. The four eastern or "Santee" (Isanti) divisions are the Mdewakanton, Wahpekute, Wahpeton, and Sisseton. Most Twin Cities–area villages in the 1700s–1850s were **Mdewakanton**.
- **Villages near Rosemount** (all 8–15 miles north or northwest, on the rivers):
  - **Kaposia**, the village of the Little Crow line of leaders. It was founded about 1750 on the east bank of the Mississippi below today's Indian Mounds Park in St. Paul. It moved to the west bank (today's South St. Paul, in Dakota County) after the 1837 treaty. Residents had to leave in 1853 under the Treaty of Mendota. More than 400 people lived there in warm months, fishing the river and farming the bottomland soils (NPS).
  - **Black Dog's village** (led by Šuŋka Sapa, "Black Dog") was on the strip of land between Black Dog Lake and the Minnesota River, where the Black Dog power plant now stands in Eagan/Burnsville. About 250 people lived there when settlers arrived. They relied on the lake and river for fish, game, and travel.
  - **Penichon's / Good Road's village** and other camps were on the Minnesota River near Mendota.
  - **Bdote**, the meeting of the Minnesota and Mississippi rivers, is a place of origin and great spiritual importance in Dakota tradition. It is about 12 miles northwest of Rosemount.
- **Rosemount itself** sits on a high, dry glacial outwash plain. At the time of the 1850s land survey it was mostly prairie and oak openings, away from the river bottoms. As far as published sources show, no major permanent Dakota village stood on the Rosemount uplands. The uplands were most likely **hunting, gathering, and travel country** for the river villages: deer, elk, prairie plants, oak-savanna nuts, and fire-managed grassland. Treat this as a reasoned inference, not a documented fact (see caveats).

### The seasonal round
The Minnesota Historical Society, the SMSC, and the Mendota Mdewakanton community describe essentially the same annual cycle:

| Season | Where people were | Main foods and work |
|---|---|---|
| **Late winter–spring** ("moon of sore eyes" to "moon of sugar-making") | Winter villages broke up. Women, children, and elders went to **maple sugar camps (čhaŋháŋpi)**. Men hunted and trapped. | Sugar maple, and also boxelder, sap collected with spiles into troughs or birch-bark vessels and boiled into syrup and sugar. Sugar was stored in birch-bark or rawhide (parfleche) containers and used all year. Men took muskrat and other fur animals and fished through the ice. |
| **Summer** | People regrouped at the **planting villages** on the river terraces (Kaposia, Black Dog, and others). | Women planted and tended **corn, beans, and squash** in hilled fields. Men fished (spears, weirs, hooks, nets), hunted, and traded. Families gathered berries such as juneberry, strawberry, raspberry, and chokecherry. In late spring and early summer they dug **tíŋpsiŋna (prairie turnip, *Pediomelum esculentum*)**, which was peeled, braided, and dried. |
| **Late summer–fall** | Harvest at the villages, then **rice camps** at lakes and sloughs. | Corn was harvested, dried, and cached in pits. **Psiŋ (wild rice, *Zizania*)** was gathered by canoe: one person poled while another bent stalks over the canoe and knocked the grain loose with sticks. The rice was then dried, parched, hulled by treading, and winnowed. Other foods were plums (kaŋte), chokecherries (čhaŋpa, pounded with dried meat for wasna/pemmican), hazelnuts (uma), black walnuts (hma), acorns, and the tubers of **mdo (groundnut, *Apios americana*)** and arrowhead (*Sagittaria*). |
| **Fall–winter** | Families scattered to their **hunting grounds**. | The **fall deer hunt** was the main source of winter meat and hides. **Bison** were hunted on the open prairies to the west and south of the Minnesota River. They were already scarce east of the Minnesota River by the early 1800s. Fur trapping (muskrat, beaver, mink) supplied trade with the American Fur Company at Mendota. Winter villages lived on stored corn, rice, dried meat, and sugar. |

Women did most of the farming, gathering, food processing, hide tanning, and lodge construction. Men did most of the hunting, fishing, travel, and protection. Sharing and kinship obligations (*wótakuye*) spread food across the community, so a bad harvest in one place did not mean famine.

### Tools and techniques
- **Agriculture:** bone, antler, and later iron hoes; hilled corn, often interplanted with beans and squash; storage pits (caches) lined with grass or bark; scaffold drying of corn and squash.
- **Wild rice:** canoes, push poles, knocking sticks, tying rice stalks into bundles before harvest to claim a stand, parching kettles, treading pits, birch-bark winnowing trays.
- **Hunting:** bow and arrow, later guns from the fur trade; communal drives; deadfalls and snares for small animals; ice fishing; fish spears and weirs.
- **Sugaring:** cut or tapped trees, wooden spiles, bark buckets, troughs, and later iron kettles from traders.
- **Dwellings:** bark lodges (*thípi tháŋka*) at summer villages; hide or later canvas tipis for hunting and winter camps.
- **Fire:** Indigenous burning in southern Minnesota kept prairies and oak savannas open. It made forage for deer, elk, and bison, improved travel and visibility for hunting, and encouraged berry and nut plants. MNopedia states that lightning alone could not have kept up Minnesota's oak savannas, which were partly a human-made landscape. Rosemount's original prairie and oak openings were very likely part of this fire-maintained mosaic. Today the SMSC runs prescribed burns on its own lands.

### Before the Dakota: archaeology of Dakota County
The National Park Service's *River of History* summarizes the Mississippi corridor through Dakota County:
- **Paleoindian (about 12,000–8,000 years ago):** small mobile bands hunting big game, later mainly bison. A Late Paleoindian spear point was found at the Sibley House / American Fur Company site (21DK31) in Mendota.
- **Archaic (about 8,000–2,500 years ago):** broader hunting, fishing, and gathering. Lee Mill Cave (21DK2) near Hastings had an Archaic hearth containing fish bones.
- **Woodland (about 2,500–350 years ago):** pottery, burial mounds, intensive use of river resources, and early gardening of squash and native seed plants. The **Sorg site (21DK1)** at Spring Lake (Nininger/Hastings area, about 10 miles east of Rosemount) had a Middle Woodland village with Hopewell-style pottery. It is also the type site for Late Woodland "Nininger" ceramics. Mound groups such as Schaar's Bluff at Spring Lake Park Reserve still survive.
- **Oneota (about 1,000–300 years ago):** corn–beans–squash farmers in southeastern Minnesota. Along this stretch of river they seem to have used mostly temporary camps (Oneota material at Lee Mill Cave).
- Very little archaeology has been published for **Rosemount Township** specifically. tDAR lists only a 1976 historical sketch of one section of the township. The densest sites are on the river bluffs and terraces, not the interior upland.

### How it ended here
- **1837 treaty:** the Dakota ceded land east of the Mississippi, and Kaposia moved to the west bank.
- **1851 Treaty of Mendota:** signed August 5, 1851, at Pilot Knob in Mendota by Mdewakanton and Wahpekute leaders. They ceded their remaining lands in Minnesota Territory, including all of today's Dakota County, in exchange for annuities. The payments were to come as interest on $1,410,000, and much of the money was diverted or delayed. MNHS notes that Little Crow and others signed only after promises about unpaid earlier treaty funds, and those promises were not kept. The bands were moved to a narrow reservation along the upper Minnesota River in 1853–54, and Euro-American (largely Irish) settlement of Rosemount began within a few years.
- **1862:** late annuities, trader fraud, hunger, and loss of land led to the **U.S.–Dakota War of 1862**. Afterward, 38 Dakota men were hanged at Mankato. About 1,600 Dakota noncombatants were held through the winter of 1862–63 in a camp below Fort Snelling at Bdote, about 12 miles from Rosemount, where hundreds died. In 1863 Congress abrogated the treaties and expelled the Dakota from Minnesota.
- **Return and continuity:** Dakota families quietly remained or returned, and some lived on in Mendota. The four federally recognized Dakota communities in Minnesota were later reestablished: Prairie Island and Lower Sioux in the 1930s, Upper Sioux in 1938, and Shakopee Mdewakanton in 1969. The Mendota Mdewakanton Dakota Tribal Community, which is not federally recognized, is based about 10 miles from Rosemount. Today the SMSC (about 20 miles west) runs Wozupi Tribal Gardens, taps its own sugarbushes, and leads land restoration with prescribed fire.

## How to find and verify this yourself
1. **Confirm whose land it is.** Go to **native-land.ca**, type "Rosemount, Minnesota," and click the point. It should show *Wahpekute* and *Mdewakanton* (Dakota/Očhéthi Šakówiŋ) territory. Native Land Digital itself says its map is a starting point, not an authority. Then read the treaty: the **MNHS "U.S.–Dakota War of 1862" site** (usdakotawar.org) has the Treaty of Mendota text and history.
2. **Read the seasonal round from Dakota sources.**
   - *Mni Sota Makoce: The Land of the Dakota* by Gwen Westerman (Dakota) and Bruce White (MNHS Press, 2012). It is the best single book. Chapters 1–3 cover the seasonal round, place names, and villages, and the maps show villages near Bdote and Kaposia. Dakota County Library has copies.
   - SMSC's **hocokatati.org** (Hocokata Ti cultural center) has articles on spring, summer, and fall subsistence.
   - *The Sioux Chef's Indigenous Kitchen* (Sean Sherman, Oglala Lakota) and the **Dakota Wicohan** organization cover today's foodways.
3. **Locate the nearby villages.** The **Bdote Memory Map** (bdotememorymap.org) lets you click Kaposia, Black Dog, Mendota, and Bdote. The NPS Mississippi National River & Recreation Area page "Kaposia Indian Site" gives Kaposia's history and location.
4. **Check the original landscape.** Look up Marschner's *Original Vegetation of Minnesota* (1930s compilation from 1850s survey notes; MN DNR GIS and sfa-mn.org). Find Rosemount (T115N R19W / R18W) and you should see prairie and oak openings or barrens. The original **General Land Office survey notes** for Rosemount Township are in the MN Historical Aerial Photos / GLO notes collections at the University of Minnesota, and they describe the 1850s vegetation section line by section line.
5. **Archaeology.**
   - The **Minnesota Office of the State Archaeologist (OSA)** and **SHPO** hold the site files. Site locations are confidential, but you can ask about township-level summaries, and the OSA "Minnesota archaeology" education pages are public.
   - The NPS *River of History* (chapter 2) and tDAR (search "Spring Lake archaeology," "Sorg site," "Rosemount Twp.") list Dakota County reports.
   - **Never dig, collect artifacts, or disturb mounds.** Minnesota Statutes 307.08 protects burials, and the law applies on private land too.
6. **Local history.** The **Dakota County Historical Society** (Lawshe Memorial Museum, South St. Paul) and the **Rosemount Area Historical Society** have files on Kaposia, early Rosemount, and the Irish settlers who arrived after 1853.
7. **In person.**
   - Visit **Indian Mounds Regional Park** in St. Paul, looking across at the old Kaposia sites.
   - Visit **Bdote / Historic Fort Snelling** (MNHS), where the 1862–63 concentration camp is interpreted.
   - At **Spring Lake Park Reserve (Schaar's Bluff)**, mound groups are protected.
   - At **Pine Bend Bluffs SNA** you can see surviving oak savanna and prairie like the Rosemount upland once had.
   - Attend public events at SMSC or the Mendota Mdewakanton community's annual wacipi (powwow).

## Uncertainties and caveats
- **"Before you" is not "gone."** Present the Dakota as an ongoing nation with treaty rights and current land stewardship, not as a vanished prehistoric culture.
- **Exact land use on the Rosemount uplands is undocumented.** The claim that the upland was hunting and gathering ground rather than a village site is inferred from landscape, village locations, and general accounts, not from a site-specific record.
- **Dakota spellings vary.** Dakota has several orthographies: 19th-century missionary (Riggs), Gilmore's 1919 spellings, and modern University of Minnesota / Dakota Wicohan spelling. You will see *psiŋ / psin*, *tíŋpsiŋna / tipsinna*, *kaŋte / kante*, and so on. Gilmore's informants were partly Teton (Lakota) and Yankton, so check terms against a Dakota-language teacher or the *Dakota-English Dictionary* (Riggs, reprinted by MNHS Press).
- **Bison** were present historically in southern Minnesota, but by the early 1800s they were rare in the Twin Cities area. Mdewakanton bison hunting increasingly meant travel west.
- **Wild rice** in Dakota County was limited compared with northern Minnesota. Mdewakanton families also traveled to rice lakes, and rice was traded.
- Some details of tools and seasons are generalized across the eastern Dakota and may differ between villages and decades. The fur trade and missionaries (from the 1830s) changed subsistence, for example plows, iron kettles, and guns.
- Population numbers for villages come from 19th-century observers and vary between sources.
