# Name five grasses in your area. Are any of them native?

## Short answer
Five grasses you can find within a short walk of home: **Kentucky bluegrass** (lawns), **smooth brome** (roadsides, old fields), **quackgrass** (field edges, gardens), **reed canary grass** (wet ditches and stormwater ponds) and **timothy** (hayfield remnants, roadsides). All five are introduced, with a caveat on reed canary grass (below). The native answer is the tallgrass prairie set: **big bluestem, little bluestem, Indiangrass, switchgrass, sideoats grama, prairie dropseed**. Yes, some are native, but you will mostly meet them in prairie plantings, restorations and remnants rather than on lawns.

## The details

Rosemount sits at the southern edge of the St. Paul-Baldwin Plains and Moraines subsection, a landscape that was historically a mosaic of oak savanna and tallgrass prairie on level outwash (DNR). The native grasses below are what that prairie was built from. Almost everything that now fills lawns, ditches and fallow lots came from Eurasia.

| Common name | Scientific name | Status in MN | Season type | Where you would see it near Rosemount |

| Smooth brome | *Bromus inermis* | Introduced | Cool-season | Roadsides, old fields, restoration edges |
| Quackgrass | *Elymus repens* | Introduced | Cool-season | Field edges, disturbed ground, gardens |

Heights and moisture preferences for the natives are from the University of Minnesota native grass poster. Warm/cool season labels are standard agronomic classifications (the UMN poster itself does not give them), so treat that column as general knowledge rather than something taken from the cited pages.

### Why warm-season vs. cool-season matters
Cool-season grasses (bluegrass, brome) green up in early spring and again in fall and go semi-dormant in midsummer heat. Warm-season prairie grasses start growing in late May and peak July-September, then turn bronze, red and tan in fall. On a November 6 walk the prairie grasses are standing as dormant tan-to-copper clumps, which is actually the easiest time to tell them apart from sod grasses that are still green.

### The reed canary grass puzzle
Minnesota Wildflowers lists it as native but classes it as invasive. A University of Minnesota study reported by that site found that most or all populations along the state's major rivers are native and genetically distinct from European ones, but the two look identical and only genetic testing separates them. Takeaway: native is not the same as harmless, since it forms dense monocultures in disturbed wet ground.

### Kentucky bluegrass
Despite the name it is generally treated as non-native to North America (USFS FEIS). It is the default lawn grass in Rosemount and also one of the worst invaders of northern tallgrass prairie.

### Quick ID tips
- **Kentucky bluegrass:** boat-shaped leaf tip like a canoe bow; spreads by rhizomes; open panicle in June.
- **Smooth brome:** many plants show a faint W- or M-shaped crease on the leaf blade; open, slightly nodding panicle; forms dense sod-like stands.
- **Reed canary grass:** tall (2-5 ft), long thin ligule (4-10 mm, no hairs), bleached tan in winter.
- **Big bluestem:** three-branched seedhead ("turkey foot") in late summer; hairy leaf bases.
- **Little bluestem:** bundled blue-green clumps turning rust-orange in fall; fluffy white seeds in October.
- **Indiangrass:** golden-brown feathery plume; "rifle sight" ligule (two small projections at the leaf base).
- **Switchgrass:** open airy panicle; ligule is a fringe of hairs.
- **Sideoats grama:** oat-like spikelets hanging in a row along one side of the stem.
- **Prairie dropseed:** fine fountain-shaped clumps; fall color golden-orange; smells like popcorn when flowering.

## How to find and verify this yourself

1. **Walk the neighborhood.** Look at a lawn, the nearest ditch or stormwater pond, a roadside strip and any planted rain garden or prairie planting. Count what you can name.
2. **Use iNaturalist.** Open inaturalist.org, choose Explore, search "Poaceae" (grass family) and set the place to Dakota County, MN (or draw a box around Rosemount). Filter by "Native" under the filters menu: iNaturalist labels species as Native, Introduced or Invasive for your place, drawing on regional checklists. Photograph a leaf collar, seedhead and base.
3. **Check nativity with two independent databases.** Minnesota Wildflowers (minnesotawildflowers.info, under Grasses, Sedges and Rushes) shows "Origin" for each species. The USDA PLANTS database (plants.usda.gov) shows native status for Minnesota on each species profile under "Distribution". Where they disagree, trust the state-level one but note the dispute (reed canary grass, bluegrass).
4. **Key out the grass yourself.** Grass ID relies on the ligule (small flap where leaf meets stem), collar, auricles and seedhead. Use a 10x hand lens and the Minnesota Wildflowers identification tips, or the UMN Extension and MN DNR prairie guides.
5. **See remnants.** Pine Bend Bluffs SNA (dry prairie) and Hastings Sand Coulee SNA are within easy driving distance and have little bluestem and other natives. The DNR page for each SNA lists plant species.
6. **Fall is the easy season.** Dormant tall prairie grasses are conspicuous now through winter; most of the introduced sod grasses are inconspicuous.

Caveat: grasses are hard. Much lawn and ditch grass cannot be identified to species without a seedhead, so photos taken outside June-September may only get to genus on iNaturalist.

## Uncertainties and caveats
- Native status of Kentucky bluegrass and reed canary grass is contested, as above.
- I did not find a published species list specific to your neighborhood; the table reflects common species for the region and landscape, not a survey. Whether a given patch near you holds a native depends on whether it was planted (many boulevards, rain gardens and stormwater basins in Rosemount are seeded with native mixes).
- Warm/cool season labels and ID tips are from general botanical knowledge, not the cited pages.
