# When was the last time a fire burned in your area?

## Short answer
There are two ways to read this, and the honest answer differs:

- **Ecological fire.** Before Euro-American settlement, fire was a normal part of the landscape here, mostly lit by the Dakota (and secondarily lightning), keeping prairie and oak savanna open. Fire went mostly silent as the land was farmed, grazed and cut by roads, and as the Dakota were forced out after the 1851-1862 period. Today, fire comes back only as planned **prescribed burns**. The most recent documented one near Rosemount is at **Pine Bend Bluffs SNA** (Inver Grove Heights, about 5 mi north): roughly **30 acres on four bluff prairie areas**, completed within the last year, probably late 2025 or around January 2026 (the page lacks a date; the date range is my inference).
- **Wildfire / accidental fire.** I did not find a documented grass or wildfire inside Rosemount. The best-documented local fires I found were structure or industrial fires (e.g., a smoldering woodchip pile at Dakota Wood-Grinding in January 2012; a business fire on Highway 3 near County Roads 42/46, date unconfirmed). If you want a definitive "last burn on my block," you must check satellite fire detections and fire-department records (below).

Confidence is **low** because the exact dates of the latest prescribed burns and any small grass fires could not be retrieved.

## The details

### 1. Presettlement fire regime
- Minnesota's prairies, oak savanna ("oak openings and barrens") and fire-dependent woodlands burned often. Without people, the DNR-related sources say landscapes would burn roughly every **3-5 years**; the open savanna character "was maintained largely by fire." Much of the Rosemount sand plain and outwash terrace is mapped historically as prairie/oak savanna, with Big Woods (maple-basswood) forest, which rarely burned (a return interval of centuries), to the west and north of the savanna belt.
- **Who lit them:** lightning sometimes, but the Dakota and other Indigenous Peoples set most fires, to clear land, to improve forage for game such as bison, and to stimulate plant growth. When the Dakota were forced off their lands, that cultural burning ended.
- **When it stopped:** Not a single date. Fire suppression followed settlement: farming, grazing and roads (firebreaks) cut the fuel and spread. In the Rosemount area, cultivation and settlement started in the 1850s (after the 1851 treaties), so frequent landscape burning had effectively ended by about the 1860s-1880s. A UMN study of remnant Minnesota oak savannas found most oaks postdate settlement (bur oak establishment peaked late 1800s-early 1900s), consistent with fire exclusion.
- Oak savanna once covered about 10% of Minnesota and now survives on less than 0.1% of its original acreage (DNR).

### 2. Recent prescribed burns near Rosemount

|---|---|
| Pine Bend Bluffs SNA (Inver Grove Heights) | ~30 acres of bluff prairie burned in 4 areas; the FMR post says "completed last week," with the page appearing around Jan 2026 (inferred). |
| Flint Hills Resources natural area (Rosemount, ~700 ac) | Part of the same Pine Bend Bluffs complex; "controlled burns" are part of its native restoration; an Oct 7, 2025 company article credits 25 years of planned burns and invasive removal. No specific burn date. |
| Dakota County Parks (Spring Lake Park Reserve, Lebanon Hills, Whitetail Woods, Miesville Ravine, Lake Byllesby, Thompson) | The county burns in spring (about early April to late May) and fall (to late November) "as conditions allow", with the date set about a day ahead. Whitetail Woods (Empire) and Spring Lake are the closest. A Spring Lake woodland restoration (56 acres) is scheduled for a prescribed burn in **fall 2028**. |
| Vermillion Highlands (2,822 acres, Empire Twp.) | A 2015 prairie planting; the UMN page doesn't mention burning, but prairie plantings typically need fire. No burn record found. |
| UMore Park | Large UMN land base; no burn record retrieved. |

So: the most recent **documented** prescribed fire near Rosemount is the Pine Bend Bluffs SNA burn. The county parks probably burned in spring 2026 or this fall; you can confirm with the county.

### 3. Accidental fire
Rosemount firefighters respond to occasional brush and grass fires (e.g., they assisted at a 2025 Prairie Island brush fire in Goodhue County), but those are not Rosemount fires. Minnesota's wildfire season is spring (snow melt to green-up, with March-May the peak) and also dry autumns. Because I could not search NASA FIRMS or DNR incident logs from here, I cannot say when a grass fire last burned in your neighborhood. In a suburban neighborhood the likely "last fire" is a small controlled yard burn, a vehicle or a structure fire, not a wildfire.

## How to find and verify this yourself
1. **NASA FIRMS (satellite hot spots).** Go to firms.modaps.eosdis.nasa.gov/map. Search for "Rosemount, MN" or paste the coordinates (about 44.74 N, 93.13 W; confirm your own). Under "Layers" turn on VIIRS and MODIS active fires; use the date slider to the last 10 days, or click "Download" / "Archive Download" for the past 1-3+ years. Detections are 375 m (VIIRS) / 1 km (MODIS) pixels, so they catch large grass/brush fires and burn piles and prescribed burns, but not small fires. Many detections near Pine Bend refinery flares are false (industrial heat).
2. **Burned-area imagery.** In Sentinel Hub EO Browser (apps.sentinel-hub.com/eo-browser) or Google Earth Engine, compare Sentinel-2 images from before/after spring using the "NDVI" or "NBR" layer. A charred prairie shows as a dark scar for weeks.
3. **Dakota County Parks.** Phone 651-438-4660 or check dakotacountymn.gov/parks (search "prescribed burn"). Ask Natural Resources staff for burn units and dates at Spring Lake, Whitetail Woods and Lebanon Hills. They typically post notices on the day-before basis and on social media.
4. **Friends of the Mississippi River (FMR).** fmr.org lists Pine Bend Bluffs SNA events and posts a note after each burn; contact staff for exact dates.
5. **Minnesota DNR.** DNR Wildfire > "Burning restrictions" and "Fire danger" maps (dnr.state.mn.us/forestry/fire) show daily conditions and local restrictions; the DNR also lists wildfire statistics by county, and the SNA program (Pine Bend Bluffs) can confirm burn history.
6. **Rosemount Fire Department / City.** The city's open-burn permit page and the fire department can tell you about local grass-fire calls; request incident data from the department through a Minnesota Data Practices Act request (public incident reports).
7. **In-person clues.** On any prairie remnant near you, look for black, charred stems at the base, an even regrowth of grasses and flowers the following summer, and fire-scarred (cracked, blackened) bur oak bark. Bur oaks with thick, corky bark in a savanna setting are the classic fire-adapted trees. In winter, look for the clean, bare, charcoal-colored soil of a spring burn.

Caveats: A prescribed burn is a planned fire, not a wildfire, and may not count for some people's definition of "fire in your area." Satellite fire data can lag or miss small burns. Dates on web pages may be missing.


## Uncertainties and caveats
- Exact date of the Pine Bend Bluffs SNA burn is not stated on the pages retrieved (one says "completed last week" with events from January 2026 listed). Treat it as "late 2025 or early 2026."
- I did not find the date of a recent prescribed burn on Vermillion Highlands, UMore, Whitetail Woods or Spring Lake; absence of evidence here is not evidence that none occurred.
- I found no wildfire/grass-fire record for Rosemount; a small one almost certainly has occurred more recently than anything I could document.
- The presettlement fire return interval (3-5 years) is a general Minnesota figure; Margoles (2009) found that a precise presettlement interval for Minnesota oak savannas could not be reconstructed from tree scars.
- The Dakota timeline: sources say Dakota burning ended as they were removed from the land; the approximate 1860s-1880s window is my synthesis, not a quoted date.
