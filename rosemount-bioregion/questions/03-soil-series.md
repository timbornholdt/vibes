# What soil series are you standing on?

## Short answer
I could not run the official soil lookup from my research environment (the USDA Soil Data Access and Web Soil Survey servers were blocked), so I cannot name the exact map unit under your yard. What the evidence supports is this: the Rosemount area sits on a Superior-lobe glacial outwash plain, and the Dakota County Soil Survey units mapped on comparable Rosemount outwash ground are **Wadena loam**, **Estherville sandy loam**, and **Hawick gravelly sandy loam**. Wadena loam is my best single guess, with Waukegan silt loam (where a bit of wind-blown silt, called loess, caps the outwash) as the main alternative. All of these formed under tallgrass prairie, so they are dark, fertile Mollisols. Because your site is a 1990s-era subdivision, expect the top layer to have been scraped, graded, and possibly replaced, and the map may list it as an "Urban land" complex.

## The details

### Where "here" is
- This page uses central Rosemount, about 44.74 N, 93.12 W, as its reference point. Soil maps change over a few hundred feet, so look up your own lot.
- Public Land Survey System (PLSS): your parcel's legal description names its section, township, and range. Find it on the Dakota County parcel viewer. Rosemount Township was surveyed as T115N, with R18W covering the eastern part of the city and R19W the western part.

### What the surrounding soils are, and why
- Dakota County's glacial deposits around Rosemount are mainly **sand-and-gravel outwash laid down by meltwater from the Superior lobe** of the Late Wisconsin ice sheet (see question 16). The 2013 SKB environmental review for a site in Rosemount describes this outwash as the surface geology, with the first bedrock (Prairie du Chien Group) about 50-100 feet down.
- That same document lists the Dakota County Soil Survey map units on the site: Wadena loam (map unit 39B, 2-6% slopes), Estherville sandy loam (41B), and Hawick gravelly sandy loam (611C and 611D). All were described as well-drained to excessively drained.
- The MN DNR ecological subsection that includes Rosemount notes that Mollisols (prairie soils) occur on its outwash plains, while forest-formed Alfisols dominate the moraines to the north.

### Soil features you can expect on a Wadena-type site
- **Profile**: Dark (black to very dark brown) loam topsoil roughly 8-12 inches deep (the Ap and A horizons), then a brown subsoil, then abrupt change to coarse sand and gravel at about 2-3 feet.
- **Drainage**: Water passes easily through the loam and very fast through the sand, so lawns on these soils dry out in dry summers and stormwater infiltrates well. This is why Rosemount-area stormwater ponds are often infiltration basins.
- **Groundwater**: Deep. The SKB report found groundwater more than 40 feet below the surface at its site, so there is no seasonal wetness in the top few feet.
- **Farmland status**: Wadena loam on 0-6% slopes is, as I recall, typically classed as "prime farmland" and Estherville as "farmland of statewide importance" in Minnesota, but I did not verify the Dakota County ratings. Check the "Land Classifications" report in Web Soil Survey. In any case, a developed lot is no longer farmland in practice.
- **Urban disturbance**: Lots built in the early 1990s were typically stripped of topsoil, graded, compacted by equipment, then re-covered with a thin layer of stockpiled or imported topsoil and sod. The undisturbed series description may only apply 1-3 feet down, and the subsoil near your foundation is likely fill. In the official database, such areas are sometimes mapped as "Urban land-Wadena complex" or the original agricultural series.

## How to find and verify this yourself

### Method 1: USDA Web Soil Survey (authoritative, free, about 10 minutes)
1. Go to **websoilsurvey.nrcs.usda.gov** and click the green **Start WSS** button.
2. Under **Area of Interest (AOI)** on the left, open **Quick Navigation** and choose **Address**. Type your own street address, then click View. You can also use **Lat/Long** and enter 44.74, -93.12 (central Rosemount), then drag the map to your exact spot.
3. Use the **AOI tool** (rectangle or polygon icon in the toolbar) and click a small box around your yard. Small AOIs give cleaner results.
4. Click the **Soil Map** tab. The legend gives the map unit symbol, name, acres, and percent of AOI. Look for names like "Wadena loam," "Waukegan silt loam," "Estherville sandy loam," or an "Urban land" complex.
5. Click **Soil Data Explorer** > **Soil Properties and Qualities** (drainage class, hydrologic group, depth to restrictive layer) and **Land Classifications** > **Farmland Classification** to see whether it is prime farmland.
6. Use **Add to Shopping Cart** > **Check Out** to generate a PDF Custom Soil Resource Report for your records.
7. Click the **series name** in the legend (or find it at soilseries.sc.egov.usda.gov, "Series Name" search) to read the Official Series Description.

### Method 2: SoilWeb / SoilWeb app (fast, phone-friendly)
- The free **SoilWeb** app (UC Davis/USDA) uses your phone GPS and shows the soil series under your feet, including the official series description. Stand in your yard and open the app; the result will be the same SSURGO data as Web Soil Survey.

### Method 3: Look in the ground (what you can see in person)
- **Texture by feel**: Dig a hole about 12-18 inches deep in a garden bed or lawn edge (call **Gopher State One Call, 811**, at least two business days before digging). Take a pinch of moist soil and squeeze it. Gritty and falls apart = sand; smooth, holds a ribbon of 1-2 inches = loam/silt loam; sticky ribbon over 2 inches = clay.
- **Depth to sand**: Keep digging (or use a soil probe or a rebar rod). On a Wadena-type site you should hit pebbly, coarse sand somewhere around 2-3 feet, in a deep hole, a very distinct change. Fill from construction may muddy this.
- **Color**: Dark brown to black topsoil points to a prairie-formed Mollisol; a pale, brown topsoil with a gray layer under it would point to a forest-formed Alfisol (less likely here).
- **Nearby clues**: Exposed banks at the gravel mine on UMore Park, road cuts, or new construction trenches show the sand-and-gravel layer under the loam.

### Method 4: Other official sources
- **Dakota County Soil and Water Conservation District** (Farmington) can interpret soil maps and run soil tests.
- **UMN Soil Testing Lab** (soiltest.umn.edu) analyzes samples for fertility.
- Legacy printed map: *Soil Survey of Dakota County, Minnesota* (USDA SCS, 1960 edition, Series 1955 No. 10, with plates); the library catalog record is below. Older names may differ from current ones (soil series are correlated and renamed over time).
- **Dakota County GIS** may host SSURGO or soils layers on its Environment service; check the MN Geospatial Commons.

### Caveats
- Soil maps are drawn at roughly 1:12,000 to 1:24,000. Map units contain inclusions (typically up to 15-25% of other soils) and the boundary may not follow the lot lines. The map unit under your exact spot can differ from my guess.
- Urban areas may be unmapped or "Urban land," meaning the series is not given or is hidden by fill.

## Uncertainties and caveats
- **Biggest uncertainty:** I could not query SSURGO for the exact point. The series named here are inferred from the geology, the regional DNR description, and a nearby Rosemount site's mapped units. I give this low confidence for the answer to "which series is at this exact point," even though I have medium-to-high confidence in the general soil type (loamy prairie soil over outwash sand and gravel).
- The research used general Rosemount coordinates, not a specific lot.
- Prime farmland and hydrologic group ratings are from memory and are not verified for Dakota County.
- Some map unit numbers (39B, 41B, 611C/D) come from the SKB EAW's table of the Dakota County Soil Survey and may differ in the latest SSURGO release.
