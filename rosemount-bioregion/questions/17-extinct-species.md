# What species have become extinct in your area?

## Short answer
Strictly, **extinct** means gone from the whole planet, and **extirpated** means gone from a region but alive elsewhere. Rosemount's story has both:

- **Globally extinct species that lived here:** the **Passenger Pigeon** (last bird, "Martha", died Sept 1, 1914) and the **Rocky Mountain locust** (last live specimen collected in 1902, formally declared extinct by the IUCN in 2014). The locust swarmed Minnesota in the 1870s.
- **Extirpated locally (gone from southern Minnesota or the state, but not extinct):** bison, elk, Trumpeter Swan, Whooping Crane, brown/grizzly bear, Karner blue butterfly (mostly), Poweshiek skipperling, and effectively the Greater Prairie-Chicken and gray wolf in Dakota County.
- **Returned or recovering:** bison (Spring Lake Park Reserve, 2022), Trumpeter Swan (about 17,000 statewide by 2015), Wild Turkey, Bald Eagle.
- **On the edge now:** rusty patched bumble bee, Dakota skipper, Higgins eye pearlymussel.

## The details

### A. Truly extinct (global) species with a Rosemount-area connection

| Species | Extinct | Connection to the Rosemount area | Confidence |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Passenger Pigeon** (*Ectopistes migratorius*) | Last wild bird shot about 1900-1902 (a plaque in Indiana marks 1902); last captive, Martha, died at the Cincinnati Zoo Sept 1, 1914 | Flocks lived and migrated mainly in eastern North America, with some reaching Minnesota. A 2026 DNR story places nesting birds at Lake Lida, the Minnesota River at Little Rapids (1834) and Wabasha (1871), and describes market hunters shipping barrels of them by train. The largest nesting ever recorded was in central Wisconsin in 1871. The bird's roosting colonies were part of the oak and hardwood forests of the Big Woods and river valleys. I found no source naming Rosemount or Dakota County specifically. | High that they occurred in Minnesota; medium that they passed through Dakota County (it is inside the species' known range, but not documented in my sources). |
| **Rocky Mountain locust** (*Melanoplus spretus*) | Last live specimen 1902 in southern Canada; IUCN extinct (declared 2014) | Plagues struck Minnesota in 1856-57 and 1865, and the 1873-1877 swarms destroyed crops in western and southern Minnesota (Laura Ingalls Wilder's *On the Banks of Plum Creek* describes western Minnesota in 1874-75). Pioneer-era Dakota County farmers were part of that crisis. Likely cause of extinction: farming (plowing, irrigation, trampling) destroyed the egg-laying and breeding grounds in river valleys of the Rockies, so the permanent source populations collapsed. | High for Minnesota; the species' effects in Dakota County specifically are inferred from the regional record. |
| **Carolina Parakeet** (*Conuropsis carolinensis*) | Last captive died 1918; last confirmed wild sighting 1910 (Wikipedia notes AOS declared the species extinct in 1939) | Historically occurred in Iowa and Wisconsin. **I found no evidence it reached Minnesota**, so do not count it as a Minnesota extinction. | Medium-high that it is not a Minnesota species. |
| **Eskimo Curlew** (*Numenius borealis*) | Probably extinct (no confirmed sighting since the 1960s) | A migrant of the Great Plains that may have passed through western Minnesota prairies; I did not verify any Dakota County records. | Low. Mentioned only as a candidate; verify in MOU records. |

Notes on the Rocky Mountain locust: its extinction is one of the great ecological surprises, because a billion-strong swarm in 1875 was reportedly the largest recorded insect aggregation in history, yet the species vanished in about 30 years.

### B. Extirpated locally (gone here, still alive elsewhere)

| Species | Status near Rosemount | Details | Confidence |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Plains bison** (*Bison bison*) | Extirpated by the 1800s; **reintroduced** at Spring Lake Park Reserve (Dakota County) | The last wild Minnesota bison was reported in southwest Minnesota in 1879; the DNR says they were essentially wiped out by 1900. On Nov 7, 2022, Dakota County announced seven bison in fenced paddocks on 150 acres at Spring Lake Park Reserve (near Hastings), possibly growing to about 15, the first Minnesota county to reintroduce them. | High |
| **Elk** (*Cervus canadensis*) | Extirpated from southern Minnesota; small managed herds remain in the northwest | A 1980s Minnesota rare-species document lists elk (the original Minnesota subspecies) as extirpated. Modern elk persist in northwest Minnesota (Kittson/Grygla). Not present in Dakota County in the wild. | Medium |
| **Brown/grizzly bear** (*Ursus arctos*) | Extirpated from Minnesota | Listed as extirpated in the state document above. Plains grizzlies ranged on prairies to the west. | Medium-high |
| **Gray wolf** (*Canis lupus*) | Absent from Dakota County; populations remain in northern and central Minnesota | Wolves were eliminated from southern Minnesota by settlement-era persecution and bounties; Minnesota has the largest wolf population in the lower 48. Occasional dispersers can wander south. I did not find a source specific to Dakota County. | Medium (general knowledge) |
| **Cougar / mountain lion** (*Puma concolor*) | Extirpated as a resident; occasional vagrants | A 2022 video of one in northern Minnesota is noted; no established population. | Medium |
| **Trumpeter Swan** (*Cygnus buccinator*) | Extirpated, then **restored** | Last known Minnesota nest 1885; by 1932 only 69 survived in the lower 48. Restoration started in the 1960s (Hennepin Parks, 1966); more than 200 birds were released 1987-1994 and 139 more 1995-2012. Statewide counts were about 17,000 in 2015, including 270 in the five-county metro. | High |
| **Whooping Crane** (*Grus americana*) | Extirpated as a breeder | Listed with other extirpated birds in the Minnesota document. Historically nested in the western prairies. Flyover records exist (the Hastings guide mentions a 2008 flyover at Carpenter Nature Center). | Medium-high |
| **Long-billed Curlew, McCown's Longspur, Swallow-tailed Kite** | Extirpated as Minnesota breeders | Same list as above: listed as "gone from the state" since the early 1900s. Kite and curlew nested on southern/western prairies and river floodplains. | Medium |
| **Greater Prairie-Chicken** (*Tympanuchus cupido*) | Gone from Dakota County; persists in western and northwestern Minnesota | Once widespread on the tallgrass prairie and oak savanna of southern Minnesota, it declined as native prairie was plowed (only about 1% of Minnesota's native prairie survives, according to a Star Tribune piece on prairie butterflies). I did not open a source documenting the last Dakota County record. | Medium-low |
| **Wild Turkey** (*Meleagris gallopavo*) | Gone by the late 1800s; restored by DNR releases from the 1970s | Common now in the Vermillion River area. Not verified in a fetched source this session. | Medium (general knowledge) |
| **Karner blue butterfly** (*Lycaeides melissa samuelis*) | Essentially extirpated from the Twin Cities region | Only one Minnesota population remains, at Whitewater Wildlife Management Area in the southeast. A population at Cedar Creek (Anoka County) disappeared in the early 1980s. A 2019 USFWS assessment says the species has been extirpated from the Minnesota recovery unit since listing. No Dakota County records found. | Medium |
| **Poweshiek skipperling** (*Oarisma poweshiek*) | Last seen in Minnesota in 2008; feared extirpated in the state | A prairie specialist; a 2014 report says it had vanished from most known sites and about 500 remain in Michigan, Wisconsin and Manitoba. The Minnesota Zoo has bred it in captivity. | High (2014 data); update status |
| **Dakota skipper** (*Hesperia dacotae*) | Federally threatened (2014); not known in Dakota County now | Wet-mesic and dry prairie specialist; Minnesota Zoo runs an insurance breeding program. | Medium |

### C. Declining but still present nearby (not extinct)

- **Rusty patched bumble bee** (*Bombus affinis*): federally endangered since 2017 (the first bee in the lower 48 so listed); a decline of about 87-90% from its former range. Minnesota is a stronghold. A single male was found at **Pine Bend Bluffs Natural Area in Inver Grove Heights** (Dakota County), and others were found in Burnsville parks. It is plausible but unconfirmed in Rosemount.
- **Higgins eye pearlymussel** (*Lampsilis higginsii*): federally endangered freshwater mussel that historically occupied the Mississippi from Prescott, Wisconsin southward; it has lost over half its range since 1965, and zebra mussels are the worst current threat. The stretch of the Mississippi next to Dakota County (Hastings, Pine Bend, Prescott) is its historical range; current population status there needs verification at the DNR/USFWS.
- **Bobolink and other grassland birds** declined by about 1.9% per year in Minnesota (Bobolink) since 1967.

### D. Why it matters locally
Rosemount sits on former tallgrass prairie and oak savanna on glacial outwash, where bison, elk, prairie-chickens and skippers depended on open grassland and fire. Plowing and, after 1850s settlement, hunting, drained wetlands, and development removed most of that. Restoration at nearby sites (Spring Lake Park Reserve bison; Vermillion Highlands prairie seeded from 2015; Pine Bend Bluffs SNA) is a way to see which species can be recovered.

## How to find and verify this yourself
1. **Minnesota DNR Rare Species Guide.** Go to dnr.state.mn.us, search "Rare Species Guide", then browse by taxonomic group, or use the *County* search (choose Dakota). Each species page shows status (endangered, threatened, special concern), the last year recorded in the state, and a county map. If a species says "historical" or a last-observed date from the 1800s or early 1900s, it has been lost from there.
2. **Natural Heritage Information System (NHIS).** Request a data review for a specific location (the DNR has a "Request an NHIS review" process); this is how developers check for rare species at a site.
3. **Minnesota Biological Survey (MBS).** The DNR's county survey maps show Sites of Biodiversity Significance in Dakota County, including Pine Bend Bluffs, Spring Lake and other remnants.
4. **USFWS IPaC.** Go to ipac.ecosphere.fws.gov, draw or enter your location in Rosemount, and get a list of federally listed species that could be present (this will list rusty patched bumble bee zones, etc.).
5. **IUCN Red List.** redlist.org: search "Melanoplus spretus" or "Ectopistes migratorius" to read the official extinct assessments and their dates.
6. **NatureServe Explorer.** explorer.natureserve.org: search a species and view "Minnesota" status rank (SX = presumed extirpated, SH = possibly extirpated, S1-S5 for others).
7. **iNaturalist and eBird.** Search the species within Dakota County; absence of any records from the modern era supports extirpation (but absence of data does not prove it).
8. **Historical sources.** The Dakota County Historical Society and the Minnesota Historical Society (mnhs.org) hold settler accounts of passenger pigeons, wolves, elk and prairie-chickens in the 1850s-1880s. Public Land Survey notes (1850s) from the Minnesota GLO survey record "Witness trees" and the prairie/oak openings habitat.
9. **In person.** Visit Spring Lake Park Reserve to see the bison; visit the Minnesota Zoo (Apple Valley) for butterfly and bee conservation exhibits; listen for trumpeter swans at winter open-water wetlands.

## Uncertainties and caveats
- **Terminology:** "extinct in your area" is usually used loosely by quizzes; I separated extinct from extirpated so you can answer either way.
- The Minnesota rare-species document I read is from the 1980s, so those "extirpated" categories predate later reintroductions (for example Trumpeter Swan, bison) and recoveries (Whooping Crane is only an occasional visitor, not a breeder).
- I verified bison, swan, passenger pigeon, locust, butterfly, bumble bee and mussel claims in fetched pages. I did not directly verify Wild Turkey, gray wolf in Dakota County, elk or prairie-chicken details in a primary source, so they are rated lower.
- Passenger Pigeon and locust presence in *Dakota County* specifically is inferred from range and regional historical records, not from a county-level specimen record.
- Eskimo Curlew is included as a low-confidence candidate only.
- Conservation status changes; check the DNR and USFWS for the current Poweshiek and Dakota skipper listings and any new sightings.
