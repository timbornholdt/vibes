# From what direction do winter storms generally come in your region?

## Short answer
Two families of storm matter in Rosemount. **Alberta clippers** form in western Canada and move southeast or east-southeast across the Dakotas and Minnesota. **Colorado lows** (and the rarer "panhandle hooks") form on the lee side of the Rockies and travel northeast toward the Great Lakes. Between them, storms arrive out of the **northwest and the southwest**, and the cold air that follows nearly always comes from the **northwest**. Be careful with the word "from": a storm's *track* (where the low-pressure center travels) is different from the *wind direction* you feel. For Rosemount the usual wind sequence is east/northeast or southeast ahead of a big low, then strong **northwest** wind behind it.

## The details

### Storm tracks (where systems come from)
| Storm type | Where it forms | Track toward Rosemount | What it usually brings |
|---|---|---|---|
| Alberta clipper | Lee of the Canadian Rockies, Alberta/Prairies | Southeast or east-southeast across the Dakotas into Minnesota, then on to the Great Lakes | Fast-moving, large temperature swings, strong winds, light fluffy snow, ground blizzards in open country |
| Colorado low | Southeast Colorado / northeast New Mexico area | Northeast across the central Plains toward the Great Lakes | Slower, wetter; can bring the heaviest snowfalls and blizzards, affecting several states |
| Panhandle hook | Colorado / Texas-Oklahoma panhandles | Turns north or northeast toward the Great Lakes | Relatively infrequent but potentially serious |
| Arctic outbreak | Arctic Canada | Southward on northerly/northwesterly flow | Extreme cold more than snow |

Supporting details from the sources:
- The State Climatology Office describes clippers as lows that form on the Canadian Prairies and head southeast into the Upper Midwest, bringing big temperature changes, strong wind and light, easily drifted snow. It also lists other origins for storms that reach Minnesota, including southeast Colorado into the Texas/Oklahoma panhandles and central Wyoming into southern Montana.
- Clippers are most common from mid-January to mid-February. They have never been formally tracked, so no true climatology exists. As of mid-April 2026, Minnesota had counted about 35 clippers that winter, which the DNR described as the high end.
- A March 12-13, 2026 clipper moved east-southeast through North Dakota and then western and central Minnesota. It produced a 61 mph gust at the Twin Cities airport.
- Colorado-low examples: the December 26-28, 2018 storm formed in eastern Colorado and moved northeast across far southeastern Minnesota to the Upper Great Lakes. The February 6-7, 2019 storm went from southern Colorado to northern Texas and then northeast past St. Louis, Chicago and Michigan. That track would normally only hit far southeastern Minnesota, but its large circulation spread snow statewide (6.3 inches at MSP airport), followed by strong northwest winds and whiteouts.
- Track matters a lot for Rosemount's snow totals. In the March 16-17, 2023 storm, a Colorado-origin low sent its heavy band from Sioux Falls through St. Cloud to Duluth, and the airport recorded only 0.1 inch.

### Wind direction (what you feel)
- **Behind the storm:** winds swing to northwest as cold air pours in. Two of the DNR event write-ups above describe strong northwesterly winds following the snow, with whiteouts in open country.
- **Prevailing winter wind:** the University of Minnesota's Snow Control Tools site lists the most frequent (modal) wind direction at MSP airport as 310 degrees (northwest) for December through March. At the Minneapolis/Lakeville (Airlake) station, which is the closest of the listed stations to Rosemount, the modal direction is 280-290 degrees (west to west-northwest) from November through March. Direction is the direction the wind blows *from*.
- **Snow-moving wind:** at Lakeville, the directions that would blow the most snow over a season are west-northwest, then northwest, then west and north-northwest. Together those four sectors account for most of the potential snow transport (about 7,200, 6,000, 2,400 and 3,000 of 28,650 kg/m). At MSP, northwest dominates, with southeast a secondary contributor.
- **Ahead of a big low (general meteorology, not from a source above):** a low-pressure center rotates counterclockwise in the Northern Hemisphere. If its track passes south and east of you, as with many Colorado lows, you sit on the north side where winds are from the east or northeast while snow falls. Once the center passes, winds shift to the northwest. That is why heavy-snow days often begin with easterly wind and end with a northwest gale. I did not find a Minnesota agency page that states this sequence for Rosemount, so treat it as standard cyclone behavior rather than a Rosemount-specific statistic.

### What this means in Rosemount
The area is flat, open, and largely farm field and subdivision edge. That means northwest wind has a long, unobstructed fetch and drifts snow onto east- and south-facing sides of buildings, hedges and road edges. Snow fences and windbreaks here are traditionally placed on the northwest side.

## How to find and verify this yourself

### 1. Look at an actual wind rose (best evidence)
1. Go to the Iowa Environmental Mesonet wind rose tool (mesonet.agron.iastate.edu, then "Wind Roses" / `sites/windrose.phtml`). The shell I used could not load it, so check it in a browser.
2. Pick network **Minnesota ASOS** and station **MSP** (Minneapolis-St. Paul) or **FCM**/**LVN** (Flying Cloud / Airlake). Airlake (Lakeville, LVN) is the nearest airport to Rosemount.
3. Set months to **December-February** and a long date range (for example the last 20 years). Choose the "wind rose" plot.
4. Look for the longest petals. Expect the biggest ones toward west, west-northwest and northwest.
5. Optionally restrict to hours with snow reported (the tool offers filters by weather code or temperature) to see the direction during snowy times. I could not test those filters, so confirm what is offered.

### 2. Cross-check with the UMN Snow Control Tools pages
Open `snowcontroltools.umn.edu`, go to Research > Climatology > Wind Analysis, and choose MSP or Minneapolis/Lakeville. You get the modal direction by month, wind roses, and a table of snow-transport potential by direction.

### 3. Read storm summaries
- MN DNR State Climatology Office "Minnesota Climate Journal" (dnr.state.mn.us/climate/journal) has write-ups for major storms with maps of the low's track. Open several and compare the track maps.
- The NWS Twin Cities office (weather.gov/mpx) posts event summaries and the "Top snowstorms" list.
- Use the NWS surface analysis map or the Weather Prediction Center surface analysis archive to find the low's position during a storm.

### 4. Observe it
- During the next snowfall, check a wind vane or the NWS hourly forecast graph (weather.gov, enter Rosemount, MN, then "Hourly Weather Forecast Graph") for wind direction. Note it at the start of the snow and again after it ends.
- After a windy snow, look at where drifts formed relative to fences, hedges and buildings. Drifts pile on the lee (downwind) side.

### Caveats
- Wind roses show where the *wind* comes from, not where the *storm* came from. Make sure you know which one the question is asking.
- Station siting affects results. Airport sensors are in open ground, and Rosemount's local wind may differ slightly from MSP.

## Uncertainties and caveats
- There is no official statistic for "direction storms come from at Rosemount." The tracks and wind patterns above are well documented, but how they should be summed into one answer is a judgement. The most defensible one-line answer is "from the northwest (clippers and arctic air), with the heaviest snowstorms coming from the southwest and moving northeast."
- Clipper counts are informal. The DNR itself says clippers are hard to identify.
- The UMN wind pages did not state their data period, and the images could not be read through my tools. Modal values came from the page text.
- The east/northeast wind-ahead-of-a-low pattern is textbook meteorology, not shown by a source for this site.
