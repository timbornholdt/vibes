# What spring wildflower is consistently among the first to bloom where you live?

## Short answer
**Pasqueflower (*Anemone patens* var. *multifida*)**. It is a fuzzy blue-violet to white native of dry prairie hillsides and sandy soils, the habitat type Rosemount's outwash plain historically offered. It flowers very early, often before its leaves expand and sometimes with snow still on the ground. Bloom is generally late March through April; Minnesota Wildflowers gives March-May as the full span.

## The details

### Why pasqueflower is the most defensible answer
- **Habitat match.** Rosemount sits on sandy-gravelly glacial outwash that supported dry prairie and oak savanna. Pasqueflower is a plant of dry, nutrient-poor soils, prairie hillsides and south-facing slopes (BWSR; Minnesota Wildflowers).
- **Early and consistent.** It is the only wildflower in the UMN SeasonWatch Hennepin County phenology calendar that blooms in March or April: average first bloom April 17, earliest April 3 (1959), latest May 1 (1978), based on 20 records from 1959 to 1985. Those are old data; modern springs tend to run earlier, and the Star Tribune reported pasqueflowers in southern Minnesota the week of March 17 in one recent year.
- **Ecologically important.** It is an early pollen source for native bees (BWSR).

### Other candidates
| Plant | Fit for Rosemount | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Pasqueflower | Best for sandy prairie / bluff prairie | Native; late Mar-Apr, sometimes into May |
| Hepatica (*Hepatica* spp.), bloodroot (*Sanguinaria*) | Woods in ravines and bluff forests | Native woodland ephemerals, typically April; not tested here for date data |
| Marsh marigold, skunk cabbage | Wet seeps, springs, river bottoms | Native, but only in wet pockets; skunk cabbage is technically a flowering-before-leaf plant of very early spring in wet woods |
| Siberian squill (*Scilla siberica*) | Very common in lawns and yards | Non-native; blooms right after snowmelt; UMN Extension notes it naturalizes easily and can escape into natural areas |
| Dandelion (*Taraxacum officinale*) | Everywhere | Non-native; blooms from roughly April onward |

I could not verify bloom dates for hepatica, bloodroot, marsh marigold or skunk cabbage from a fetched source in this session, so I rank them on general knowledge only. If you want to rank these against pasqueflower with data, use the iNaturalist method below.

### Where to see pasqueflower near Rosemount
- **Pine Bend Bluffs SNA** (Rosemount/Inver Grove Heights): dry prairie and bluff habitats. The listing I read does not name pasqueflower specifically, so bloom there is plausible but unconfirmed.
- **Hastings Sand Coulee SNA** (Dakota County): Minnesota Wildflowers pasqueflower photos were taken here, so it is a confirmed local spot.
- **Louisville Swamp area of the Minnesota Valley NWR near Jordan** and **Richard T. Anderson Conservation Area, Eden Prairie** were named by the Star Tribune as Twin Cities-area sites. Cannon Falls prairie also appeared in an MPR feature.
- Vermillion Highlands, Lebanon Hills and Whitetail Woods are other candidates but I found no confirmation of pasqueflower there.

### ID
Flower 1-2 in across, 5-7 pointed blue-violet to white sepals, many yellow stamens, silky-hairy stem, whorl of three finely divided leaves below the flower; later a feathery seedhead. Plants 3-18 in tall.

## How to find and verify this yourself

1. **iNaturalist phenology.** Go to inaturalist.org > Explore > Observations. Search "Anemone patens" (or "Pasqueflower"), set the place to Dakota County, MN. Open the Data/Graphs view (or "Seasonality" histogram on the species page) to see the month and week of observations. Add "Flowering" under Annotations > Plant Phenology to count only flowering records. Repeat for Siberian squill, hepatica, bloodroot.
2. **USA National Phenology Network.** At usanpn.org, use Nature's Notebook / Visualization Tool, select "pasqueflower" (if available) or "Anemone patens", and Minnesota to see recorded "open flowers" dates. Not retrievable in this session.
3. **UMN SeasonWatch.** seasonwatch.umn.edu has county calendars (a PDF each for Hennepin and Washington; look for Dakota if available). Treat historical averages as old; compare with recent iNaturalist records.
4. **Scout in person.** From late March, walk a south-facing, sandy, grassy slope in a SNA or park. Last year's dried grass and silvery buds low to the ground are the cue; flowers open on sunny days above roughly 45-50 F. Use caution: do not dig or pick plants in SNAs or county parks (illegal and harmful); photograph instead.
5. **Keep your own record.** Visit the same spot every few days from March 15 and note first open flower date. This is exactly what phenology networks do, and the results are valuable to compare year to year.
6. **Report your sightings** to Nature's Notebook or iNaturalist.

Caveats: the first-bloom date varies by about four weeks, depending on winter snow, the March warm spell, and slope aspect. One observation does not equal "consistently first"; check several years in iNaturalist.


## Uncertainties and caveats
- The only quantitative first-bloom dates I verified are the SeasonWatch Hennepin County figures (1959-1985 data); Dakota County-specific numbers were not found.
- Site-specific presence of pasqueflower at Pine Bend, Vermillion Highlands, Lebanon Hills and Whitetail Woods is unconfirmed.
- Early-spring "first flower" can also be a skunk cabbage or silver maple/elm flower (wind-pollinated trees) if you count non-showy blooms.
